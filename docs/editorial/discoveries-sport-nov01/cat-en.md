A small spotted cat can be easy to overlook in a family famous for lions and tigers. In Bolivia, the overlooked animal helped expose a gap in the scientific map of South American cats. Researchers have described Leopardus tilcayo, a distinct lineage from the Yungas forests. The discovery is a recognition of an existing wild animal, not the sudden appearance of a new one.

## How DNA revealed the tilcayo

The Current Biology study compared whole genomes from 38 cats, including eight museum specimens. It resolved five species within the tiger-cat complex and described L. tilcayo from Bolivia. Museum DNA was especially useful for checking historical identities, making the research a combination of present-day sampling and a fresh reading of old collections. [Lescroart et al., Current Biology (2026)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960982226011103)

That approach matters because similar coats can conceal different evolutionary histories. A photograph may suggest that a cat deserves closer study, but it cannot by itself establish a species. Genome-wide comparisons test whether the proposed animal belongs to an independently evolving lineage, rather than relying on one striking marking or an unusually small individual.

## A discovery that also recognises local knowledge

The National Geographic Society, which supported participating explorer Paola Nogales-Ascarrunz, identifies the cat with Bolivia’s Yungas cloud forest and documents the sanctuary animal that helped draw scientific attention. Its scientific name retains “tilcayo”, a locally used name. Being new to formal taxonomy does not mean being unknown to the people who share an animal’s habitat. [National Geographic Society, discovery account (September 2026)](https://news.nationalgeographic.org/photos-new-wild-cat-species/)

The discovery team’s university describes the finding as the first formal description of a newly named living cat species in more than a century. The qualification matters. Scientists have revised other cat classifications during that period, including restoring older names or separating previously grouped populations. The century claim should not be read as a century without any changes to cat taxonomy. [PUCRS, research team announcement (18 September 2026)](https://hmg-portal.pucrs.br/noticias/pesquisa/nova-especie-de-felino-leopardus-tilcayo/)

## Five lineages do not mean five brand-new discoveries

The study supports four previously proposed species alongside the newly described tilcayo, and also recognises a Peruvian subspecies of L. tigrinus. Those are different taxonomic outcomes. Counting every supported lineage as a newly discovered species would exaggerate the result. The achievement is a clearer family tree and a distinct name for an animal that did not fit it.

## Why a name matters for conservation

Recognising a species changes the unit that conservation must assess. A population once treated as part of a widespread cat may need its own survey of distribution, habitat and threats. Genomes alone do not supply a current population count. The next questions concern where tilcayos live, how connected those populations are and which pressures they face.
