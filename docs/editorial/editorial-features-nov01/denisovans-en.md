A fragment of bone can preserve a molecular clue long after its owner has vanished. For Denisovans, an extinct human group first recognised through genetic evidence, that clue is helping connect names to actual skeletons. New studies of ancient proteins are expanding the fossil record. They reveal ancestry and anatomy more securely than they reveal the face of a particular individual.

## What can an ancient protein tell us?

Palaeoproteomics examines surviving protein fragments, including the order of their amino acids. Certain sequence differences can help distinguish evolutionary lineages when researchers compare them with suitable reference data. Proteins do not provide an entire genome. Their value is that identifiable fragments can sometimes persist where recoverable ancient DNA is scarce. [European Research Council, protein identification of Penghu 1 (2025)](https://erc.europa.eu/news-events/news/denisovans-extinct-human-species-once-lived-east-asia-fossil-shows)

This makes the method a bridge between molecular biology and the fossil drawer. A bone may look broadly human yet remain difficult to assign. A diagnostic protein variant can sharpen that assignment. The strongest interpretation combines several strands of evidence, including preservation, laboratory controls, geological context and comparisons with other specimens.

## Five identified remains from Bianfu Cave

A Nature study published in September 2026 assigned two teeth, two skull fragments and a partial radius from Bianfu Cave in Yunnan to Denisovans. The radius is a forearm bone, making it especially valuable beyond the better-known teeth and skulls. Five remains should not be mistaken for five independently identified people. [Rao et al., Nature (9 September 2026)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10976-9)

The Chinese Academy of Sciences reports a mixture of anatomical characteristics in the radius rather than a simple copy of either a Neanderthal or a modern human arm. That is an important shift in the story. Denisovans become a group whose physical variation can be investigated, rather than a genetic label attached to a handful of familiar fragments. [Chinese Academy of Sciences, Bianfu research account (2026)](https://english.cas.cn/newsroom/research-news/202609/t20260906_1192641.shtml)

## Xujiayao adds a clearer view of the skull

An October 2026 Nature paper analysed proteins from two Xujiayao specimens and compared the assemblage’s anatomy with other ancient humans. Molecular evidence supported Denisovan affiliation. The study then described a combination of cranial, facial and dental features, providing a stronger basis for comparing fossils previously assigned uncertain evolutionary positions. [Xing et al., Nature (21 October 2026)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11132-z)

A molecularly identified fossil and a second fossil that resembles it are not the same strength of evidence. Anatomical similarity can support a wider hypothesis, but it should not silently turn every proposed relative into a DNA-confirmed individual. The emerging picture is richer precisely because researchers can now compare independent clues rather than force every specimen into one familiar category.
