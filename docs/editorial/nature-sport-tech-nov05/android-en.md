The announcement that changed Google’s place in mobile did not unveil a phone. On 5 November 2007, the company presented Android and a coalition of partners who intended to build around it. That distinction was the point. A single handset could attract attention; a shared software platform could give many manufacturers and developers somewhere to begin. The consequences unfolded over years, but the public proposition was already clear: mobile computing could be organised around an ecosystem.

## 5 November 2007: a platform takes the stage

In Google’s original blog post, mobile-platforms director Andy Rubin explicitly distinguished the announcement from the much-rumoured Gphone. Android was presented as a package of operating system, interface and applications developed with industry partners. The post also cautioned readers that phones would come later. Treating this date as the first day a consumer could buy an Android handset confuses a platform announcement with a retail launch. [Google: the Android announcement, 5 November 2007](https://googleblog.blogspot.com/2007/11/wheres-my-gphone.html)

## The alliance behind the ambition

The Open Handset Alliance’s release named 34 founding companies, spanning manufacturers, carriers and technology suppliers. HTC, T-Mobile, Qualcomm and Motorola were among them. The strategy addressed a coordination problem: a mobile platform needed hardware, networks, software tools and applications to work together. Android’s promise of openness was also a commercial proposal, intended to reduce barriers to developing devices and services. These were the partners’ stated ambitions, not proof that every later Android product would be identical or equally open. [Open Handset Alliance: the 2007 announcement](https://www.openhandsetalliance.com/press_110507.html)

## 2008: the first phone becomes tangible

On 23 September 2008, Google introduced the first Android-powered phone, the T-Mobile G1, developed with HTC and T-Mobile. The announcement gave the earlier platform plan a recognisable consumer object. It also put developers and applications at the centre of the story. Android’s historical significance cannot be reduced to the appearance of that first device: the larger wager was that a common foundation could support many different phones and experiences. [Google: the first Android-powered phone](https://googleblog.blogspot.com/2008/09/first-android-powered-phone.html)

## The source-code milestone

A further date belongs in the timeline: 21 October 2008, when Google and the alliance announced the platform’s public source-code availability through the Android Open Source Project. Announcement, first handset and source release were separate milestones. Open-source software also does not mean that every service, commercial arrangement or application on a finished phone is part of the same open code. Keeping those distinctions clear explains the achievement more accurately than a story about one miraculous launch day. [Open Handset Alliance: source-code release, October 2008](https://www.openhandsetalliance.com/press_102108.html)

The turning point was a change in how a phone could be built and extended. A handset became an entry into a wider relationship between manufacturers, developers, network operators and users. Android did not invent the smartphone, but its shared-platform approach helped reshape the choices available around it. The day mattered because of what many people would subsequently build.
