Before YouTube became a destination, online video was a problem to solve. Recording a clip and getting someone else to watch it were separate tasks, often divided by incompatible software and awkward transfers. Three founders helped close that gap. Their achievement was to make moving images feel like an ordinary part of the web, with consequences far beyond the original upload button. [YouTube: original 2005 funding announcement](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/youtube-receives-35m-in-funding-from/)

## Who founded YouTube?

Chad Hurley, Steve Chen and Jawed Karim founded YouTube in 2005 after working at PayPal. In Sequoia’s retrospective, Chen recalls the uncertainty around delivering video through the emerging broadband web. The three names matter: a company remembered through a single brand began with collaborators confronting a practical engineering and product problem. [Sequoia Capital: YouTube, with founder Steve Chen](https://sequoiacap.com/podcast/crucible-moments-youtube)

YouTube’s own historical account dates the website’s registration to 14 February 2005. Registration, the first upload and the wider public launch are different milestones. Collapsing them into a single birthday makes a tidy story but hides how a product becomes usable in stages, through experiments, revisions and growing demand. [YouTube: the website’s registration](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/youtube-at-15-my-personal-journey/)

## An elephant clip, rather than a grand manifesto

The first upload, Me at the zoo, arrived on 23 April 2005. The 18-second clip was filmed at San Diego Zoo. Its ordinariness is the interesting part: a short observation could become something worth sharing without a studio, a broadcaster or an elaborate production. The threshold for publishing a moving image had shifted. [YouTube: the first upload](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/celebrating-10-years-of-youtube/)

## Why sharing mattered as much as watching

The early proposition was not simply a library of clips. YouTube’s 2005 announcement described uploads, tagging and sharing across other sites, blogs and email. A video could travel through a conversation already happening elsewhere. This distribution logic helped turn a viewing page into a participant in the wider web, rather than an isolated destination a viewer had to remember to visit. [YouTube: the original sharing proposition](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/youtube-receives-35m-in-funding-from/)

## Google’s acquisition changed the scale

On 9 October 2006, Google announced an agreement to buy YouTube for $1.65 billion in stock. The original release, preserved in an SEC filing, said the service would retain its identity. That date marks the announcement of an agreement, not the completion of every legal step. The significance was the combination of a fast-growing video community with a much larger technology company. [Google: acquisition announcement filed with the SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1288776/000119312506206884/dex991.htm)

## Creators became part of the business model

The YouTube Partner Program began in 2007, introducing revenue sharing with eligible creators. This was a crucial change in incentives. A channel could become work, with an audience to serve and production costs to meet. Eligibility and earnings are separate questions: the existence of a programme never meant that every upload would produce an income. [YouTube: Partner Program history](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/introducing-expanded-youtube-partner/)

Copyright was another structural challenge. Content ID launched in October 2007, according to YouTube’s retrospective explanation. The need for rights-management machinery reveals the tension inside an open publishing system: the ease of sharing does not establish permission to reuse. A successful platform has to manage that difference as well as it manages playback. [YouTube: Content ID’s October 2007 launch](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/content-id-and-fair-use/)

## From a video archive to a system that chooses

In its 2021 technical explanation, YouTube said recommendations generated more viewing than subscriptions or search. Its account describes signals including watch time and satisfaction surveys. This is the company’s explanation of its own system, not an independent audit. The broader editorial question remains: when a platform helps decide what comes next, its choices become part of the viewing experience. [YouTube: its recommendation system, explained in 2021](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/on-youtubes-recommendation-system/)

Shorts began its early beta in India in September 2020, another sign that the platform would keep adapting its formats. Yet the founding question still feels familiar: how easily can a person make something and find an audience? YouTube’s history is the story of removing that obstacle, then discovering the responsibilities created by the answer.
