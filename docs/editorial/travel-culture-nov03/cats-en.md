A city portrait usually begins with its skyline. In Istanbul, a cat can quietly take over the foreground. That reversal helps explain the appeal of the city’s feline reputation: monumental history becomes a backdrop to an animal’s ordinary afternoon. Yet the most interesting story is not that cats make attractive photographs. It is that people repeatedly make room for lives they do not entirely own or control.

## Neighbours rather than scenery

Ceyda Torun’s documentary Kedi offers one carefully observed window into that relationship. Istanbul Modern identifies the 2016 film and its neighbourhood cats, including animals followed in Galata, Cihangir, Feriköy and Kuzguncuk. Its value is the attention it gives particular cats and their human neighbours. A documentary portrait can reveal relationships; it cannot establish a complete census or prove that every district shares an identical attitude. [Istanbul Modern, Kedi film programme](https://www.istanbulmodern.org/en/film/cat)

The film’s own presentation describes cats living between domestic and independent worlds. That is a useful cultural idea, provided it does not erase the differences between individual animals. A familiar street cat may have several regular carers without living inside anyone’s home. Calling such an animal family expresses a relationship and a responsibility. It does not automatically tell us who arranges treatment, pays a bill or notices when it disappears. [Kedi, official film presentation](https://www.kedifilm.com/about)

## The work behind the affection

Care also has an institutional side. Istanbul Metropolitan Municipality’s veterinary service describes vaccination, sterilisation, treatment and animal registration among its activities. That makes a more substantial picture than the romantic image of a city where kindness alone solves everything. A bowl or a photograph is visible immediately; clinical care and administrative follow-through are harder to capture. Both the human connection and the practical infrastructure belong in the story. [Istanbul Metropolitan Municipality, veterinary services](https://tarim.ibb.istanbul/veteriner-hizmetleri-mudurlugu/hakkimizda.html)

A recent example comes from Fatih Municipality. Its announcement of 31 October 2026 describes transport support for residents arranging sterilisation for groups of at least three street cats. This is a specific district programme, not a promise of the same service across Istanbul. Its significance is the connection between residents and professional care. Shared affection becomes useful when someone can turn concern into an organised next step. [Fatih Municipality, street-cat care programme (31 October 2026)](https://www.fatih.bel.tr/tr/main/news/fatihte-sokak-kedilerinin-kisirlastirilmasina/11767)

## A city is more than its most charming image

Family is therefore best read as a cultural metaphor, not a statement that every cat is safe or every resident agrees. Reducing the relationship to a single legend, a religious generalisation or a spectacular population estimate makes it easier to repeat and harder to understand. We give no unverified citywide cat count here. The more revealing question is how an individual animal becomes recognisable enough that its welfare matters to people around it.

## Looking without turning animals into props

The editorial lesson is to keep the animal at the centre of the encounter. A satisfying photograph should not become the measure of whether the meeting mattered. Watching quietly, respecting distance and listening to people already responsible for an animal offer a different kind of attention. The supplied hero is an editorial depiction, not evidence of these cats’ identities or care. The city’s strongest feline story is the continuing relationship beyond the frame.
