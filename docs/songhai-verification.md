## Evidence and distinctions

- Gao is the principal political capital. Gomez (1990), Hunwick (1996), and Nobili's historiographical discussion distinguish its power from Timbuktu's commercial and scholarly role. Their differing interpretations of scholarly influence are attributed, not collapsed into a single consensus.
- Cissé and colleagues' Gao Saney research and Takezawa/Cissé's palace interpretation distinguish early urban archaeology from retrospective dynastic traditions. No invented founding date or complete early royal genealogy is provided.
- Sunni Ali's reign, 1464–1492, and Askia Muhammad's reign, 1493–1528, are drawn from academic chronologies. The University of Cape Town dates Timbuktu's conquest to 1468. Some modern museum material uses 1469; the chosen chronology is explicitly identified. Djenné's conquest is conservatively dated to the 1470s because modern accounts differ over dates and siege details.
- Askia's pilgrimage in 1496–1498 is separate from Mansa Musa's earlier Mali pilgrimage. UNESCO dates Gao's Tomb of Askia to 1495. The article does not repeat a contradictory suggestion that the 1495 monument was built after return from the 1496–1498 pilgrimage.
- UNESCO dates the visible Djenné mosque reconstruction to 1906–1907. It is not presented as an untouched Songhai-period structure.
- The invaders were Saadians. Ahmad al-Mansur ordered the expedition, Judar commanded it, and Askia Ishaq II's forces faced it at Tondibi. Nobili (2022) gives 13 March 1591; Kaba dates the turning point to March 1591.
- Kaba's 1981 paper, not its later online publication date, is identified accurately. Its extract and scholarly notes support attention to commercial motives, Mediterranean politics, variable troop figures, scholarly exile in 1594 and protracted resistance, analyzed for 1591–1612.
- Exact army ratios, unverified dialogue, cattle-stampede reconstructions, unsupported casualty totals and claims that firearms alone explain every subsequent change are omitted.
- Al-Sa'di's seventeenth-century chronicle is identified as retrospective. Hunwick's edition is offered as a scholarly reading resource, not described as a complete manuscript independently transcribed by PRESDA. Nobili documents Moroccan manuscript copies and a conquest announcement; royal rhetoric is distinguished from independent verification.
- Manuscript collections cover multiple eras. No present-day manuscript count is presented as a sixteenth-century census. Ahmad Baba's 1615 manuscript concerns legal reasoning about slavery and is not represented as an abolitionist or demographic survey.
- No invented GDP, modern-dollar imperial fortune, total population, gold tonnage or universal gold/salt price is supplied. The economy graphic shows commodities and relationships without fabricating quantitative flows.

All 21 reading sources are listed in the article and corresponding localizations. Some publisher full texts are access-restricted; bibliographic records, accessible abstracts, excerpts and the university and UNESCO pages were used within their evidence limits. No claim is made to have inspected unavailable archives or interviewed historians.
